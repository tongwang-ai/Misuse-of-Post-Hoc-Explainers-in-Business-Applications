\paragraph{Discussion1}
            Our experiment showed that LIME and SHAP were not able to correctly order features in order of importance and on average constructed rankings that were generally not strongly correlated with the true ranking and sometimes even negatively correlated with the true ranking. The unsatisfactory quality of these rankings, as we will argue throughout the paper, is due to a combination of deficiencies spread across the data, model, and explainer being used. For example, because the accuracy of $\mathcal{M}$ was less than $100\%$, it is essentially impossible for explanations of $\mathcal{M}$ to be fully faithful to $\mathcal{G}$. $\mathcal{M}$ is trained to find features that are good for prediction as the most important, not to find features that are important to $\mathcal{G}$. 
                            
            The practical implication of these results is that when managers attempt to use LIME or SHAP to find which factors in their decision-making process are the most important and the least important, they will usually be misled. In our loan application example, this could result in a rejected applicant putting in an extreme amount of unnecessary effort to obtain a higher-paying job because an explainer gave undue importance to income. Or, in a hypothetical marketing context, the incorrect importance ranking could look like LIME or SHAP suggesting that ad spending was the most important factor to customer churn when actually product prices were most important. Money could be lost from ad campaigns that cost comparatively more than, for example, offering a sale or discount.

Consider a scenario where managers are uncertain whether online advertisement spending increases the likelihood of purchasing a product or not because some market research suggested that consumers find them vexing.             \paragraph{Discussion3} 
        Managers may only want to find the handful of features that are most important to their business, especially when many features are involved. Even if they want to rank all the features, it is still critical that most important ones are identified as such. So, the fact that LIME and SHAP cannot reliably find, e.g., the top 3 features in our simple loan approval scenario should urge business researchers to use LIME and SHAP with caution for this purpose when stakes are high. Again, part of the reason for this must be that $\mathcal{M}$ places importance on features which are good for prediction, not features which are important to $\mathcal{G}$. Thus, the most important features to $\mathcal{M}$ are the ones that are the most useful for prediction and are not necessarily the same ones that are the most important causal drivers of the phenomena underlying the data.
